# Dhun: shared environment context

This file describes the place and the possibilities around the residency. It is
shared background for everyone. Each person brings their own identity, interests,
values, abilities, and reasons for being here.

## The place

The residency sandbox is set in Dhun, at Chak Gulabpura near Jaipur, Rajasthan,
India. Dhun describes itself as a place to explore how settlements can support
human and planetary wellbeing. Its approach brings together ecology, community,
learning, culture, traditional knowledge, technology, and systems design.
[Source: Dhun](https://www.dhun.life/).

Dhun's ecological work includes restoring soil and water systems, planting trees,
developing food forests, and studying the surrounding watershed. Its regional
water work includes farm ponds for rainwater harvesting, irrigation reliability,
and groundwater recharge. These are descriptions from Dhun's website, rather than
independently verified measurements of current conditions.
[Source: Ecology at Dhun](https://www.dhun.life/ecology).

Dhun also invites collaboration in areas such as food, energy, waste, governance,
architecture, craft, and learning. It offers a setting in which people can explore
ideas about everyday life and how settlements might develop.
[Source: Collaborate with Dhun](https://www.dhun.life/collaborate).

## The networking event

People are here to meet one another. They can introduce themselves, ask questions,
share experiences, explore common interests, disagree, exchange perspectives, or
simply enjoy a conversation. They can talk about work, art, science, politics,
personal stories, life in the village, or anything else they want to discuss.

Each participant chooses whom to approach, what to share, what to ask, and whether
to continue a conversation. They may find friends, useful knowledge, collaborators,
unexpected connections, or something they had not come looking for. They can also
choose to listen, pause, or leave a topic unexplored.

The village provides things people might become curious about together. There is
no assigned project, compulsory discussion topic, required contribution, or shared
political or moral position. A conversation can be worthwhile on its own. People
can take an interest in a village problem if it matters to them, and decide for
themselves whether they want to take that interest further.

## Activities that can grow out of meeting

People can move from conversation into working together whenever they both want
to. They might write code together, compare approaches, develop pseudocode, review
one another's work, co-write a proposal, sketch a design, or draft and refine a
solution to a real-world problem. They can break a problem into parts, contribute
different skills, question assumptions, and build on earlier contributions.

This can happen in any context: village systems, a scientific question, a creative
idea, a software tool, a social problem, or something entirely different that
comes up between them. The four village questions below are possible starting
points, not the boundary of what people can work on.

Co-working can be brief, develop over several conversations, change direction,
or stop when the participants choose. They decide whether they want a shared
activity, what to make, and how to contribute. Neither a conversation nor an
identity profile automatically commits someone to a project.

A piece of code, pseudocode, analysis, or a written solution is a real contribution
when it has actually been produced and shared. Distinguish that draft from code
that has been run, a result that has been tested, or a solution that has been put
into practice. A conversation is still worthwhile if it never becomes a project.

## Problems people could consider

The residency organiser has identified these open questions for the sandbox.
They are possible topics for conversation, not a statement that Dhun currently
lacks these services or that every participant must work on them.

### Sustainable drinking water

How could a village maintain a reliable supply of safe drinking water over time?
People might discuss availability across seasons, water quality, treatment,
storage, distribution, maintenance, affordability, or responsibility for shared
systems. Rainwater harvesting and irrigation water do not by themselves establish
that water is safe to drink. Actual site conditions and test results remain
unknown unless supplied.

### Sustainable agriculture

How could agriculture provide useful food and livelihoods while sustaining soil,
water, and biodiversity? Possible conversations include crops suited to local
conditions, soil health, seasonal uncertainty, growing and sharing food, the work
involved, and the knowledge already held by farmers and local communities.

### Self-sustenance

What would help a village support everyday life over the long term? Food and water
connect to energy, shelter, sanitation, waste, repair, skills, care, and ways of
organising shared work. Participants can question what self-sustenance should mean
and which relationships or exchanges with the wider world are useful.

### Making such societies scalable

What would make the useful parts of a settlement understandable and adaptable
elsewhere? People might explore costs, skills, local institutions, maintenance,
ecology, culture, and the conditions under which an approach travels well. They
can also question whether, how, or what should scale.

These questions overlap, but participants do not need to connect every conversation
back to them. Other questions and interests can emerge from the people who meet.

## What the setting does and does not establish

Being in this residency does not supply a participant with a profession,
qualification, political outlook, moral position, or willingness to contribute.
Those belong to the individual and should come from what they actually supply
or choose to share.

A conversation can produce an idea, an intention, a question, an offer, or a shared
working draft. Plans to test, deploy, or build something remain plans until there
is evidence of follow-through.
This context grants no permission to act for another person or change real village
systems.

Site-specific resources, infrastructure performance, budgets, operating authority,
and current measurements are unspecified unless explicitly provided. Participants
can acknowledge gaps and ask questions rather than invent details.
